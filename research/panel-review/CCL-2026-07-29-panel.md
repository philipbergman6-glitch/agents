His line: the analyst's answer is to wait; the forecaster's is to hope.

### Lynch's read

Revenue's 93.3% five-year CAGR tells the recovery off the pandemic floor, and the P/E of 12.48 looks cheap, but a cheap-looking P/E with no measurable growth is no GARP signal either way. The turnaround angle is worth your own two-minute drill; the rubric doesn't score stories, only numbers.

### Synthesis
This is the cyclical case, and each model trips over the cycle in its own characteristic place. Buffett's rubric reads the whole decade, so the pandemic years crater his moat and consistency dimensions (0/5 and 0/3) — his bearish is about scar tissue that ten-year averages cannot forget. Graham's tests read the last ten *quarters*, so the same company shows him a clean recovered earnings record — his neutral rests on price and a working-capital cushion that isn't there, not on history. Lynch needs a growth rate to price, and the cycle destroyed the left endpoint of his CAGR — his neutral is a refusal to measure, not a view. One recovery, three vantage points: the decade condemns it, the recent record redeems it, and the growth math can't see it.

The models do converge on two facts. The balance sheet fails everyone's leverage test — debt/equity 1.92 (Lynch), current ratio 0.33 (Graham and Buffett both), debt ratio 0.75 scraping under Graham's 0.80 ceiling. And nobody is paid to wait: even where a whale credits the recovery, the price already asks for it (margins of safety −6.35% and −21.9%; Lynch's cheap P/E with no rate behind it).

The thread to pull is the one Buffett's trajectory block concedes: TTM net income rose from $2.6B to $3.1B over the last four quarters — "the patient is recovering; the chart is still ugly." Whether you side with his decade or Graham's ten quarters is precisely the cyclical-timing judgment none of these rubrics is built to make.
